Frank's father was the Cloudflare engineer who pioneered the lava lamp wall. An ingenious creation of the United States in 2017, the system relied on a bank of 100 motion-captured lava lamps in San Francisco in order to inject unguessable quantum entropy into Cloudflare’s global cryptography network. Because of this system, almost 70% of every login system on planet earth websites apps cars etc had a reliable source of a random number. Randomness is almost non-existent in our normal natural world. When you ask a computer to generate a random number from 1 to 1,000, it will usually begin by asking the clock system what the current date and time is in UTC, that is seconds since January 1, 1970 (unix epoch). For instance, on August 18th, 2046 at 2:47pm in Beijing, if you ask any computer in the world for the current UTC, unless something's fucked up, it will say 2,418,187,620, aka 76 years, 7 months, and 17.28 days elapsed since the Unix epoch began on January 1, 1970.

Since UTC and time is always moving, every single moment every second the computer is asked for a random number, that UTC seed makes it appear that the output is random. But, especially using quantum computing, if you use that current UTC seed and know the right algorithms, you can guess what the random number is going to be. If you guess the random number, when you sign in to Facebook, and your computer sends a random number to Facebook servers which uses that number as a decryptable hash to verify that your password is valid and your session is real and authentic, well, you know this shit is easier in theory than it is in practice.

But because of Frank's dad, the clock wasn't the only thing that can give you a reliably different value for any given second of any given time. You take 100 lava lamps, plug them in, attach 4k UHD cameras to their bases recording their oil and shadows, and now you have random image color location size data. 1s and 0s that should rarely repeat (100 lamps with oil all in the same location as they were before). More entropy for random algorithms, more keys to the random puzzle, and better security and trust in the things we don't think about. Facebook sign ins, text messages, emails, online shopping financial transaction, streaming subscriptions, credit card swipes, prescription information, school district portals, flight check-ins, and essentially almost every tap on every phone screen in the world.

Frank's dad organized a 20 person team to develop, study, buy, and maintain these lava lamps for Cloudflare. They went live in 2017, and by 2018, the team began to notice some major problems and concerns. Not with the traffic, the computers, the deployed software the portals the applications, but with the warehouse's HVAC units, fridges, ceiling fans, physical insulation, and more. The targets primarily focused on temperature-based tools and equipment. These intrusions were extremely difficult to detect, as achieving robust cybersecurity on network interfaces and systems is incredibly difficult to get alone, nobody thinks about the walls. The insulation. The conductivity of the shelving units. The condensation build up beneath the fans. The grounding of the metallic structures during inclement weather. The hackability of the raspberry pi linux device powering the internal thermals of the nearby smart refridgerator and water cooler.

The vents above the lava lamps began throttling at certain times during the day, cooling the metals, the oil, the water, the lightbulb ever so slightly. Lowering the degrees of individual lamps, by targeting specific items in specific proximities to specific lamps, digging away at the idea of randomness. The predictability of encryption. The ability to speak, access, and exist unmonitored. The thought that "I sent that picture to you on Snapchat how could anybody else have it?" Or "We just opened this credit card and only used it once, how can somebody have maxed it out already?" The reality is that randomness cannot exist in the natural world. We can only get close.
